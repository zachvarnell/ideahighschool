Harmonics of the plucked string

Level at pickup is the loudness of each harmonic in the sound, compared with the loudest one. A harmonic is missing when the string is plucked at a point where that harmonic has a node (no motion).

Nearby strings that start to ring

A string rings by itself when a harmonic of the plucked string has almost the same frequency as one of its own harmonics. This is resonance. The spacing rule used for the size of the effect is a classroom model (see About).

The five strings

The drum head: its frequencies

A round drum head vibrates in patterns. Mode (m, n) has m lines across the head and n rings. Frequencies are the lowest frequency times a ratio from the zeros of Bessel functions. Tap weight shows how strongly your last tap started each pattern. Taps at the centre only start the ring patterns (m = 0).

Harmonics of the plucked string

Head boost is how much the drum head raises each harmonic. Level in sound is the loudness in the sound, compared with the loudest harmonic.

Other strings that start to ring

After a pluck, a string rings when one of its harmonics matches a harmonic of the plucked string. After a tap, a string rings when one of its harmonics is close to a head frequency. Sizes come from classroom model rules (see About).

About: equations, model rules and limits
  • Wave speed on a string: v = √(T ÷ μ). T is tension in newtons. μ is mass per metre. Longest wave on the string: λ = 2L. Fundamental f₁ = v ÷ λ = (1 ÷ 2L) √(T ÷ μ). You choose the note, and the page works out the tension.
  • Harmonics: fₙ = n × f₁ (a little higher for a stiff string). A string plucked at a point 1/n of its length from an end has no harmonic n.
  • Pluck shape: a triangle with height h at position p. Size of harmonic n: bₙ = 2h ÷ (n² π² p (1 − p)) × sin(n π p). Energy in the pulled string: E = T h² ÷ (2 L p (1 − p)). The page also adds up the energy of the harmonics as a check.
  • Guitar sound follows the speed of the string at the pickup position. Banjo sound follows the speed of the string at the bridge, where the strings push on the head.
  • Stiffness: B = π³ E d⁴ ÷ (64 T L²), with E = 200 GPa for steel. Wound strings use a core diameter of 0.4 × string diameter (estimate). Fading rate of harmonic n: (0.8 + 0.015 n²) per second, divided by the sustain setting. Teaching values, not measured.
  • Spacing model (classroom rule): coupling between two strings = 0.2 ÷ (1 + (d ÷ 12 mm)²). A ringing harmonic has size = coupling × resonance factor × size of the driving harmonic. The resonance factor depends on how close the two frequencies are (quality factor 300). On a real instrument the strings share a bridge, so the real effect of spacing is smaller than this rule shows.
  • Banjo head model: a round, thin, evenly stretched membrane of radius 139.7 mm (11 inch). Its frequencies are f(m,n) = f₀₁ × j(m,n) ÷ 2.4048, where j(m,n) are zeros of Bessel functions. Tension per length: Tₘ = σ (2π R f₀₁ ÷ 2.4048)², with σ = 0.26 kg/m² for a plastic head (estimate). A tap at distance r and angle θ from the centre starts pattern (m, n) with size ∝ Jₘ(j r ÷ R) cos(mθ) ÷ Jₘ₊₁(j)², multiplied by 1 ÷ (1 + (f ÷ 400 Hz)²) because a finger touches the head for a short time (estimate). Each pattern fades with rate π f ÷ 15 per second (quality factor 15, estimate).
  • Head boost on strings: boost(f) = 1 + 1.6 × Σ c(m,n) ÷ √(1 + ((f − f(m,n)) ÷ bandwidth)²), where c(m,n) = |Jₘ(j r_b ÷ R)| is how well the bridge (12 mm from the centre) couples to the pattern. Bandwidth = f(m,n) ÷ 30. A string harmonic near a head frequency is louder. This is a classroom model, not a measurement.
  • Strings ringing after a tap: size = 0.02 × head pattern size × a closeness factor that depends on the gap between the string harmonic and the head frequency.
  • Banjo strings: 5th string starts at the fifth fret and is about 74.9 % of the scale length; the other four run nut to bridge. Gauges follow a common 10-13-20w-29w-10 set (w = wound).
  • Limits: strings move up and down only; strings are drawn parallel (real banjo strings fan out slightly); no fretting; no resonator or body; drum head modes use an ideal membrane (no air loading); the pictures are stretched up and down by the stated factors; fading values do not match any one real instrument.
  • FFT: a Fourier transform of 65536 samples (strings) or 8192 samples (taps) with a Hann window. "Live" graphs use the browser's own analyser while the sound plays.
  • Sources: Fletcher and Rossing, The Physics of Musical Instruments (2nd ed., Springer, 1998); Fletcher, H. (1964) Normal vibration frequencies of a stiff piano string, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 36; Bessel function zeros from standard tables; equal temperament with A4 = 440 Hz; steel density 7850 kg/m³.
